Week 01 · Laboratory

Types of AI: match the method to the problem

Look at the job, the kind of answer needed, and the evidence available. Then choose the approach that best fits.

Session 1 · 2 December 2026 Lab reference · self-paced Work in pairs

Laboratory learning objectives

  • Identify the method behind everyday software
  • Train a two-class image classifier
  • Test predictions on unseen examples
01

Use three clues

There is no need to guess from the product name. Read what the system is asked to do.

What is the output?A category, number, group, plan, action, or new piece of content?
What evidence is available?Fixed rules, labelled examples, unlabelled data, or feedback?
What clue points to the method?Say the clue out loud before selecting an answer.
02

Your approach cards

Use these short definitions while you work. These are overlapping parts of a toolkit, not mutually exclusive types of AI. The same real system can combine more than one approach; choose the main fit described in each scenario.

R

Rules and automation

Apply a fixed condition or sequence written in advance.

C

Classification

Use labelled examples to choose a category for a new case.

#

Regression

Use examples to estimate a numeric value.

G

Clustering

Group similar items when no group labels are given.

P

Search and planning

Compare possible actions to find a plan under constraints.

↗

Reinforcement learning

Learn actions by trying them and receiving rewards or other feedback.

Aa

Generative AI

Create new text, images, audio, code, or other content.

03

Match the seven situations

Choose one main approach for each situation. Judge each independently; options can be reused. Explain your clue to your partner, then check all seven choices. The first check gives hints for mismatches; the second reveals the intended answers and reasons. You can change choices and retry or start over.

1 · Classroom lights

At 19:00, turn on the classroom lights if the sensor reads below 300 lux. The threshold is fixed.

2 · Check a leaf photo

Use a collection of photos labelled “healthy” or “diseased” to classify a new leaf photo.

3 · Estimate energy use

Use past meter readings and weather data to estimate how many kilowatt-hours the school will use tomorrow.

4 · Find groups of songs

Group songs by tempo and other audio features. No mood or genre labels are supplied in advance.

5 · Plan a delivery route

Choose a route for a van visiting four addresses before a deadline while avoiding a closed road.

6 · Train a warehouse robot

In a simulation, a robot tries actions to reach a charging station. It earns points for a fast, safe arrival and loses points for hitting an obstacle. Over repeated trials, it uses those rewards to improve its action choices.

7 · Draft a lesson summary

Given a teacher’s outline and lesson notes, produce a first-draft summary for students to review.

This is practice, not a graded test. Your choices are not saved; reloading clears them. Record your final explanation in your own notes.

Pair prompt: Before checking, tell your partner one clue for each choice. A good explanation names the output and the evidence—not just the approach name.

04

One more question

Choose one scenario and discuss with your partner:

What might the system get wrong?

Think about poor or missing data, a new situation, a badly chosen reward, or a plausible but inaccurate generated answer.

How could a person check it?

Name a practical check. For example, inspect the source photos, compare an estimate with actual readings, review the route constraints, or verify a draft against its notes.

Finish with a one-sentence explanation.
“I chose ___ because the problem asks for ___ and provides ___.”

05

Extension: image classification with Teachable Machine

Train a small image model at Teachable Machine using two everyday objects. Do not collect classmates' faces, voices or private information.

  1. Collect varied labelled images of each object.
  2. Train the model and test five unseen examples.
  3. Record the predicted class, confidence and actual class.
  4. Describe what happens when light or background changes.

Confidence is not a guarantee of correctness.

Teacher guide · 60-minute club session

Use selected method-matching cards (around 10 minutes), then a short Teachable Machine object demo (around 10 minutes) during the last part of the one-hour session. The complete seven-card lab is a self-paced extension. Students should describe a predicted class, a confidence score and a failure case.
