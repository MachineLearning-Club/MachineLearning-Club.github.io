Probability and Expected Value — Advanced

Optional mathematical extensions: conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, expected value and interpreting evidence.

Mathematical reference

Let X be the face of a ten-sided die, with possible values 1 to 10. When the die is fair, all ten faces have probability 0.1.

Formulas

Equally likely outcomesP(A) = |A| / |Ω|
Conditional probabilityP(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
Bayes' theoremP(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B)
Expected valueE[X] = Σ x · P(X=x)
VarianceVar(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²

Worked example — a rigged die with a deceptive average

A ten-sided die has probabilities P(1) = P(10) = 0.30 and P(2) = … = P(9) = 0.05.

E[X] = (1 + 10) × 0.30 + (2 + ⋯ + 9) × 0.05 = 5.50

A fair ten-sided die also has E[X] = 5.50, but the two distributions are different. Equal expected values do not imply equal distributions.

Problems

1. Conditional probability

Roll a fair ten-sided die. Define A = “the roll is 2” and B = “the roll is even”.

  1. Write down A, B and A ∩ B.
  2. Calculate P(A), P(B), P(A ∩ B) and P(A | B).
  3. Explain the meaning of the denominator in P(A | B).
Hint

The even-number condition leaves five possible faces.

Show solution

a) A = {2}, B = {2,4,6,8,10}, A∩B = {2}. b) P(A) = 1/10, P(B) = 1/2, P(A∩B)=1/10, P(A | B) = (1/10)/(1/2) = 1/5. c) It represents the restricted set B in which the result must lie.

2. Who knew the answer?

A student knows the answer to a true/false question with probability 0.4; otherwise, the student guesses. Knowing guarantees correctness, while guessing has probability 0.5 of correctness.

  1. Calculate P(correct).
  2. Find P(knew | correct) using Bayes' theorem.
  3. Repeat if the prior P(knew) is 0.8. Explain how the prior changes the answer.
Hint

P(correct) = P(correct | knew)P(knew) + P(correct | guessed)P(guessed).

Show solution

a) 1 × 0.4 + 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.7. b) 0.4/0.7 = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714. c) P(correct)=0.8+0.1=0.9 and P(knew | correct)=0.8/0.9=8/9 ≈ 0.8889.

3. Compare two expected values

For a fair ten-sided die P(x) = 0.1. For a loaded die P(1) = 0.55 and P(2) = … = P(10) = 0.05.

  1. Check that each distribution sums to 1.
  2. Calculate E[X] for both dice.
  3. Find the expected sum after 30 independent rolls of each die.
Hint

Use E[X] = Σ xP(x) and E[X₁+⋯+X₃₀] = 30E[X].

Show solution

a) 10×0.1=1 and 0.55+9×0.05=1. b) Fair E[X]=5.5; loaded E[X]=0.55+0.05×54=3.25. c) Fair: 165; loaded: 97.5.

4. Same mean, different spread

Compare the fair ten-sided die with the tricky die from the worked example.

  1. Show that both have expected value 5.5.
  2. Calculate E[X²] and variance for each die.
  3. Which distribution is more spread out?
Hint

For the fair die, the sum of squares 1² + 2² + … + 10² is 385. Use Var(X) = E[X²] − E[X]².

Show solution

a) Both E[X] = 5.5. b) Fair E[X²]=38.5 and Var(X)=38.5−30.25=8.25. Tricky E[X²]=0.30×(1²+10²)+0.05×(2²+…+9²)=44.5 and Var(X)=44.5−30.25=14.25. c) The tricky die has larger variance, because extreme values are much more likely.

5. Use Bayes' theorem to investigate a die

A seller says that 20% of their ten-sided dice are loaded. The loaded type has P(1)=0.55; a fair die has P(1)=0.10. You choose one die at random and roll a 1.

  1. Calculate the probability of observing a 1.
  2. Find the posterior probability that the chosen die is loaded.
  3. Why does observing one 1 not prove that the die is loaded?
Hint

Let L = “die is loaded” and R = “roll is 1”. Use P(L | R) = P(R | L)P(L)/P(R).

Show solution

a) P(R) = 0.55×0.2 + 0.10×0.8 = 0.19. b) P(L | R) = (0.55×0.2)/0.19 = 11/19 ≈ 57.9%. c) A fair die can also produce 1, so the evidence is informative but not decisive.

Weighted sums of probabilities

The overall chance of an event can be found by considering all the distinct ways it could happen. The weights are the probabilities of entering each situation; the numbers being averaged are the chances of success within those situations.

Law of total probabilityP(S) = Σ P(S | Rᵢ) × P(Rᵢ), when the routes Rᵢ cover all cases without overlap
Weighted averageE[X] = Σ pᵢ × xᵢ, where all weights are nonnegative and Σpᵢ = 1
Affine transformationE[aX + b] = aE[X] + b
Sum of two variablesE[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] (even if the variables are dependent)

6. A weighted probability: two delivery routes

A delivery company uses Route A for 70% of its parcels and Route B for the remaining 30%. A parcel arrives on time with probability 90% on A and 60% on B.

  1. Draw two branches, each with a route probability and a conditional on-time probability.
  2. Compute the overall probability of arriving on time.
  3. Explain why your result must lie between 60% and 90%.
Show the worked solution

0.70×0.90 + 0.30×0.60 = 0.63+0.18 = 0.81. The result is an 81% weighted probability. It is a convex combination of 60% and 90%, so it cannot be outside that interval.

7. Why weighted averages behave predictably

A game gives 0 points with probability 0.20, 5 points with probability 0.30 and 10 points with probability 0.50.

  1. Check that the weights add to 1, then compute E[X].
  2. If the teacher doubles every score and adds 2 bonus points, find E[2X+2] without recomputing the full table.
  3. Can the original expected score exceed 10 or be negative? Explain using the nonnegative weights.
Show the worked solution

a) 0×0.20 + 5×0.30 + 10×0.50 = 6.50. b) E[2X+2] = 2×6.50 + 2 = 15. c) No: the mean is a weighted average of values between 0 and 10, using nonnegative weights summing to 1.

Building variance from the idea of an average

The expected value gives the centre of a distribution. But it does not explain whether most outcomes stay near that centre or lie far away. Variance measures the average squared distance from the mean.

From an average to a spread:

  1. Compute the weighted centre: μ = E[X] = Σ pᵢxᵢ.
  2. Measure the deviation of each outcome: xᵢ − μ.
  3. Square deviations so negative and positive distances do not cancel.
  4. Average those squares: Var(X) = Σ pᵢ(xᵢ − μ)².

Equivalent shortcut: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])².

E[X] = 5; Var(X) = 25.

8. Does the same mean imply the same variance?

Compare Distribution A: X is always 5, with Distribution B: X is 0 or 10 with equal probabilities.

  1. Find the expected value of both variables.
  2. For each variable, calculate the squared deviations from its mean.
  3. Find each variance and explain what “spread” means here.
  4. Use the slider above to investigate the new variance when P(X=10)=0.25.
Show the worked solution

Both means are 5. A has variance 0 (always at the mean). B has variance 0.5(0−5)² + 0.5(10−5)² = 25. With P(10)=0.25, the mean is 2.5 and variance is 0.75(0−2.5)²+0.25(10−2.5)²=18.75.

Covariance — when two quantities change together

Variance describes the spread of one variable. Covariance describes the joint movement of two variables: do they tend to be above their averages at the same time, or in opposite directions?

Cov(X,Y) = E[(X−E[X])(Y−E[Y])]

= Σ pᵢ (xᵢ−μₓ)(yᵢ−μᵧ) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y].

E[X] = 0.50; E[Y] = 0.50; Cov(X,Y) = +0.25.

9. Calculate covariance using a tiny joint distribution

Compare three equally likely sets of pairs: A {(0,0),(1,1)}; B {(0,1),(1,0)}; C {(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)}.

  1. Find E[X] and E[Y] for each set.
  2. Find E[XY] and use Cov(X,Y) = E[XY]−E[X]E[Y].
  3. Explain what the sign of covariance means for each set.
  4. Why does covariance zero not always prove independence for arbitrary distributions?
Show the worked solution

All three sets have E[X] = E[Y] = 0.5. A: E[XY]=0.5, covariance +0.25. B: E[XY]=0, covariance −0.25. C: E[XY]=0.25, covariance 0. Independence implies zero covariance (when defined), but zero covariance alone does not generally imply independence.

Connection to machine learning

Probability models uncertainty. Bayes' theorem updates beliefs when evidence arrives. Expected value gives a weighted average, but different distributions can have the same average. This is why checking only a model's mean output can hide important differences.
