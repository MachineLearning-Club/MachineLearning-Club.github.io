4.1. First decide what problem you are solving

Core idea

Known desired answers → supervised prediction. No class labels → unsupervised grouping. The algorithm is selected according to the question and the information available, not according to which graph looks attractive.

QuestionWhat you already haveAppropriate algorithm
“Will tomorrow's outdoor activity go ahead?”Weather observations and past yes/no answersID3 (learn a tree)
“Where do the delivery locations naturally form two groups?”Coordinates, no group labelsK-means (find centroids)
“What class does a new player resemble?”Coordinates of players with known classesK-nearest neighbours (compare labelled examples)
Quick check — is K-means a classifier?

Not by itself. Cluster identifiers are arbitrary and not supplied as correct answers. To call a new location “yes” or “no” you need an independent, meaningful labelling step, which is a different problem.

4.2. Reconstruct ID3 from the school-activity diary

Recall Week 2: twelve records with six yes and six no outcomes, and input features sky, wind, temperature. Each of the three sky groups contains four rows.

Can you reconstruct the first question?

  1. Before: H([6+,6−])=1 bit.
  2. After Sky: sunny [4+,0−] H=0, cloudy [2+,2−] H=1, rainy [0+,4−] H=0.
  3. Weighted average: 4/12×0 + 4/12×1 + 4/12×0 = 1/3.
  4. Gain: 1−1/3=2/3, greater than Wind (~0.082) and Temperature (0).
  5. Finish: Sunny→yes, Rainy→no; for Cloudy ask Wind: Calm→yes, Windy→no.

Practice first without a solution: sketch the four leaves, label positive/negative counts and trace cloudy / windy / warm. Now reveal the tree one step at a time.

Step 1 / 4

Why can temperature safely disappear from this tree?

In the recorded data, once sky and (if necessary) wind are known, warm versus cold does not separate the labels further. The conclusion depends on this particular training dataset, not on a universal claim about temperature.

4.3. Reconstruct K-means from the five-point example

Without labels, use A(−1,0), B(1,0), C(0,1), D(3,0), E(3,1) and initial centroids μ₁=(−1,0), μ₂=(3,1). The objective is to keep the total squared distance to assigned centroids small.

Repeat the arithmetic from memory

  1. For B(1,0), squared distances are 4 and 5; B joins cluster 1.
  2. First groups: {A,B,C} and {D,E}.
  3. Updated means: μ₁=((−1+1+0)/3,(0+0+1)/3)=(0,1/3); μ₂=(3,1/2).
  4. The squared-error sum decreases from 7 to 19/6≈3.167.
  5. On reassignment, membership remains unchanged: convergence.

Why are the groups not the same kind of answer as the ID3 leaves?

ID3 leaves predict an already-defined target label (yes/no). K-means produces a partition without a target label. Choosing K=2 does not prove that two real-world categories exist.

4.4. One new idea: nearest-neighbour voting

KNN is a short preview, not a third full lesson. Suppose six known players are points, with three class A examples (1,1), (1,2), (2,1) and three class B examples (4,4), (4,5), (5,4). We want a label for a new player Q=(2,2).

Worked prediction with K=3

  1. Squared distances to A points: 2, 1, 1.
  2. Squared distances to B points: 8, 13, 13.
  3. The three nearest records are all class A: (1,2), (2,1), (1,1).
  4. Majority vote predicts A for Q. Unlike K-means, the neighbours have known labels.

Potential confusion: the letter K occurs in both names, but K-means uses K groups while KNN uses K neighbours. That distinction matters more than the notation.

4.5. Check your model fairly

Training accuracy asks how well a model matches examples already used to construct it. A separate test set asks how well it predicts records it has not used to learn. Do not allow test answers to influence training.

Compute a small confusion matrix

A toy model is evaluated on six previously withheld cases. The actual/predicted labels are:

CaseActualPredicted
1yesyes
2yesyes
3yesno
4nono
5nono
6noyes

Taking yes as the positive class: TP=2, TN=2, FP=1, FN=1. Accuracy = (TP+TN)/6 = 4/6 ≈ 66.7%. A high training score could coexist with a lower test score.

Adjust two mistake counts

Why should this tiny percentage not be reported as a reliable real-world accuracy?

Six test examples are far too few to establish generalisation. The sample is fictional, and we have not checked whether it represents other days or students. The fraction is for practicing the calculation only.
