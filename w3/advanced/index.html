Week 03 · Advanced

Unsupervised Learning: K-Means

From moving points to explaining K-means

These optional problems extend the five-point numerical exercise with more careful distance calculations, an argument for why means minimise squared error, and experiments showing the influence of initialisation and outliers.

Formulas

Squared Euclidean distanced²(P,μ) = (Px−μx)²+(Py−μy)²
Centroidμ=(Σx/n, Σy/n)
Total squared errorJ=Σ d²(point, assigned μ)
Two-centroid boundaryAll points equally distant from the two centroids lie on their perpendicular bisector

Reasoning exercises

1. Calculate a full K-means iteration on paper

Use A(−1,0), B(1,0), C(0,1), D(3,0), E(3,1), initial centroids μ₁=(−1,0), μ₂=(3,1).

  1. For B, C and D, compute both squared distances.
  2. Write the clusters after the first assignment.
  3. Find the mean coordinates of each cluster.
  4. Calculate J before and after the centroid update.
Hint

Use squared distance; there is no need to take square roots when deciding which centroid is nearer.

Worked solution

B: 4 versus 5; C: 2 versus 9; D: 16 versus 1. Clusters are {A,B,C} and {D,E}. New centres: (0,1/3), (3,1/2). Initial J=7, after update J=19/6. This follows Ciortuz, §7.1.2, solved problem 7.

2. Why is the mean the best centre for squared distance?

Three delivery locations lie at x=0, x=2 and x=4 on a line. Test a candidate collection centre at x=1, x=2 and x=3.

  1. For each candidate, calculate the sum (0−c)²+(2−c)²+(4−c)².
  2. Find the mean of the three x coordinates.
  3. Observe which candidate gives the smallest sum. Can you explain the connection?
Worked solution

At c=1 the sum is 1+1+9=11; at c=2 it is 4+0+4=8; at c=3 it is 9+1+1=11. The mean is (0+2+4)/3=2, the best candidate. For a general set, expanding Σ(xᵢ−c)² shows it is the smallest when c equals the mean of xᵢ.

3. What does the separator between two centroids look like?

Place centroids at μ₁=(0,0) and μ₂=(4,0). Any point P=(x,y) lying exactly on the boundary is equally far from both.

  1. Write the equality of squared distances.
  2. Cancel common terms and solve for x.
  3. Sketch the resulting separator and decide which side belongs to which cluster.
Worked solution

x²+y²=(x−4)²+y², hence 0=−8x+16 and x=2. This is the vertical perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the centroids. The nearest-centroid regions are separated by a straight line, not a curved boundary.

4. Can a strange starting position change the result?

Four points are arranged at (0,0), (0,2), (8,0), (8,2), with K=2.

  1. Try starting centroids at (0,0) and (8,2). Follow the assignments and updates.
  2. Try starting centroids at (0,0) and (0,2), using the same tie-breaking rule throughout.
  3. Compare the resulting groups and cost. Explain what you notice.
Hint

Some first assignments can create a horizontal grouping before later updates. Record every intermediate grouping; do not assume the clusters are visually obvious to the algorithm.

Worked guidance

The first start naturally separates left and right, giving centres (0,1) and (8,1) and J=4. Starting at (0,0) and (0,2) instead groups the bottom two points and the top two points: means (4,0) and (4,2), with J=64. Neither assignment changes on the next iteration. The algorithm has reached a worse stable arrangement than the left/right grouping (J=4); its result depends on the starting centres.

5. Why do outliers distort a mean?

Three x-coordinates are 0, 1 and 2. Now a fourth faraway coordinate, 20, appears.

  1. Calculate the original mean.
  2. Recalculate the mean including the outlier.
  3. Compare the median with the new mean. Which is less affected by the distant point?
  4. Would ordinary K-means necessarily ignore that outlier? Justify your answer.
Worked solution

Original mean: 1. With 20: (0+1+2+20)/4=5.75. Median of 0,1,2,20 is (1+2)/2=1.5, much less affected. Because K-means uses means and squared distance, an outlier may pull a centroid substantially away from the main cluster.

Connection to the previous week

ID3 uses label uncertainty to choose a split. K-means uses geometric distance to choose a centre. The two algorithms both work iteratively but solve different learning tasks: one predicts a known label, while the other discovers groups without labels.
