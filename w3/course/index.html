1. No labels: a different learning problem

Real-life question. A map contains ten delivery coordinates but none are assigned to a district.

Unsupervised algorithms look for structure in unlabelled data. K-Means groups points by proximity to representative centres.

Worked example.

The same six points could be split into two clusters without any human-provided A/B labels.

points = [(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),(8,7),(8,8),(9,8)]
print(points)

2. Distance and centroid

Real-life question. A courier needs a representative centre for nearby addresses.

The centroid is the coordinate-wise average of points in a cluster. Euclidean distance measures straight-line separation.

Worked example.

For (1,1),(2,1),(2,2), centre=(5/3,4/3)≈(1.67,1.33).

group = [(1,1),(2,1),(2,2)]
print(tuple(sum(p[i] for p in group)/len(group) for i in (0,1)))

  1. Average x coordinates
  2. Average y coordinates
  3. Mark the new centre

3. The K-Means loop

Real-life question. Initial centres are placed at (1,1) and (9,8).

Repeat assignment to the nearest centre, then update each centroid, until assignments or centres stop changing.

Worked example.

After one round, the first three points cluster near (1.67,1.33) and the other three near (8.33,7.67).

4. What K-Means cannot tell you

Real-life question. Two groups of homes are clustered, but one road between them is closed.

Distance alone cannot account for every real-world constraint. Different K or random starts may change clusters; clusters are not true labels.

Worked example.

A U-shaped group can be split unnaturally by simple distance-to-centroid decisions.

print('K = 2 is a choice, not an automatically correct answer')
