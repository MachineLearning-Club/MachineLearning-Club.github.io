1. Predict before opening the model

Go back to the twelve-row activity diary. Do not start by writing code. Start by noticing what the examples have in common.

  1. How many training records say yes and how many say no?
  2. Look at the four sunny records. Are their answers identical?
  3. Look at the four cloudy records. Which other feature separates the two answers?
  4. Write your guess: what question should go at the top of the tree?
Reveal the initial observations

There are six yes and six no labels. All four sunny records are yes. The cloudy group contains two yes (calm) and two no (windy). Sky is the useful root question; Wind is useful for the remaining cloudy records.

3. Build a full tree, one step at a time

Step 1 / 4

  1. Stop at the two branches where all training labels agree.
  2. For the mixed cloudy branch, find the better remaining question.
  3. Trace cloudy → windy and cloudy → calm to the final predicted labels.

5. Test a prediction the model did not see

The finished tree predicts sky=cloudy, wind=calm → yes. Try sky=rainy, wind=calm. Then imagine a never-seen value sky=foggy.

Explain: what does “unseen” mean? Why is a warning more useful than a random guess?

Research (3p) (optional)

Invent four extra rows for the weather diary. First make them agree with the original patterns, then change one label so the dataset becomes noisy. Compare how much the root gain changes and whether the constructed tree becomes more complicated.

Suggested investigation
  1. Copy the original notebook, preserving the first run.
  2. Add or modify one example at a time.
  3. Record the gain for all features, not just the best one.
  4. Write two sentences explaining whether the chosen root remains unchanged.
