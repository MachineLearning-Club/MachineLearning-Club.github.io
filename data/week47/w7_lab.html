1. Recall why a single neuron cannot learn XOR

Without looking up the answer, fill in the XOR outputs for inputs (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1). Sketch four points in a square, colour the positive examples, and attempt to draw one separating line.

Reveal the reasoning

The two positive points lie at opposite corners of the square. The two negative points occupy the other corners. One straight line cannot place the two positive points on the same side and both negative points on the opposite side.

2. Compute every hidden neuron output

Our two-layer network computes OR=step(x₁+x₂−0.5), AND=step(x₁+x₂−1.5), then XOR=step(OR−2×AND−0.5).

  1. Trace input (1,1). Write both hidden weighted sums and the output sum.
  2. Trace input (1,0) and verify a different output.
  3. Repeat for all four rows; compare with the XOR truth table.

Checkpoint: the shown weights are supplied by the teacher; we have demonstrated representation, not learned them automatically yet.

3. See why a smooth output is useful for training

Use a one-input sigmoid neuron with x=1, starting weight w=0, bias b=0 and target 1. Work out the output and squared loss: σ(0)=0.5, L=½(1−0.5)²=0.125.

  1. Predict the sign of the gradient before revealing it.
  2. Set η=0.4 and step through the update until the new loss appears.
  3. Choose η=0.8 and compare the one-step result. Does a larger step always guarantee better training?
